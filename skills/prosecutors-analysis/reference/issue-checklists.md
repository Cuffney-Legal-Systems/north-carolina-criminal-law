# Issue Checklists

Distilled from the ten-case evaluation in the thesis. The cross-cutting sweep
runs on every case; the charge-specific checklists run whenever the matching
offense (or an analogous one) is analyzed. Each item exists because a real
evaluating prosecutor flagged its omission.

**These are checks you run, not sections you write.** Run every applicable item
against the record — then put only the **hits** in the draft. An item that came
up clean earns no sentence, no "not applicable," no reassurance that it was
considered; the one-line "checked and clean" close in the Weaknesses section
covers all of them at once. Walking the reader through empty checklist
categories is a leading cause of a fifteen-page draft.

**A hit is reported in one or two lines, with the report's own words.** Bold
label, the problem, the quote. *"An orange sweater" (Doe 1) vs. the red hoodie
booked at arrest* does the work of a paragraph. Save prose for a hit that is
genuinely case-dispositive, and flag that one at the top of the report.

## Cross-cutting sweep (every case)

**Identification integrity** *(Cases 1, 5, 9)*
- [ ] Compare every witness description (clothing, build, race, age, hair)
      against the defendant's documented appearance at apprehension. Flag every
      mismatch — the control case's "orange sweater" description vs. "red
      hoodie" at arrest was noticed by all six prosecutors and missed by the AI.
- [ ] Identify the procedure used: show-up vs. photo lineup vs. spontaneous.
      Note show-up suggestibility explicitly when one occurred.
- [ ] Search all narratives and supplements for failed identifications. A
      witness who "did not identify any subjects" in a lineup must be reported
      even when other identification evidence is strong.
- [ ] Note viewing conditions: lighting ("dimly lit area"), distance, duration,
      "did not get a good look at the suspect's face", elapsed time between
      incident and identification.
- [ ] For forensic identification, state *where* the sample came from (DNA on
      the *outside* of a vehicle proves less than DNA inside).

**Report inconsistencies** *(Cases 5, 7)*
- [ ] Cross-check descriptions of people, vehicles, and property across every
      narrative. Where they conflict (white vs. grey/silver Nissan), report the
      conflict — never silently adopt one version.
- [ ] If the report is poorly organized or incomplete, say so in Weaknesses;
      that is itself case information.

**Statements** *(Cases 2, 3)*
- [ ] Quote legally significant suspect/witness statements verbatim. Colloquial
      phrases can carry elements ("run that" = instruction to rob = conspiracy
      intent).
- [ ] Distinguish admissions from confessions. "Admitted to knowing the truck
      was stolen" and "admitted to being in the truck" are admissions of
      circumstantial facts — not a confession to stealing it, no matter what
      the officer's narrative calls it.

**Injuries** *(Cases 4, 5, 6)*
- [ ] Never assume severity. A stab or gunshot wound is not "serious injury"
      absent facts: wound depth, treatment required, hospitalization,
      lasting impairment. If undocumented, mark it as a fact gap.
- [ ] Report visible injuries observed by officers — corroboration of the
      victim's account is as probative as the account itself.

**Physical evidence** *(Cases 1, 3, 9)*
- [ ] Was the weapon recovered? The stolen property? Projectiles/casings?
      Non-recovery goes in Weaknesses.
- [ ] Surveillance footage: exists? quality? does it actually show the
      contested fact (face, shooting, direction of movement)?

**Defenses** *(Cases 4, 6)*
- [ ] Any claim of self-defense, accident, or alibi anywhere in the report
      triggers full analysis against the defense pattern instruction — plus the
      facts that rebut it (e.g., video showing the victim walking away when
      shot rebuts "he reached into his pocket").
- [ ] Physical positions matter: the suspect on the ground *under* the victim
      is a self-defense fact even if no one uses the words "self-defense."
- [ ] Accident and self-defense are separate defenses; address each on its own.

## Charge-specific checklists

**Robbery with a dangerous weapon — G.S. 14-87 (N.C.P.I. 217.20/217.30)**
*(Cases 1, 3, 5, 7, 10)*
- [ ] Dangerous/deadly weapon must be *proven*. An airsoft pistol, BB gun, or
      air gun "resembling a firearm" is a serious proof problem — analyze it,
      don't mention it in passing. If the weapon element fails, analyze common
      law robbery as the lesser included offense.
- [ ] Intent to permanently deprive is an element — include it (evaluators
      docked summaries that dropped it).
- [ ] Life endangered or threatened: tie to specific conduct.

**Discharging a firearm into occupied property — G.S. 14-34.1 (N.C.P.I.
208.90)** *(Cases 4, 9)*
- [ ] Occupancy at the time of discharge must be established per structure/
      vehicle — a parked car is not occupied without a fact saying so.
- [ ] Defendant's knowledge or reasonable grounds to believe occupancy: look
      for facts like "he had just been inside the shop."
- [ ] Each occupied structure/vehicle is a separate count; analyze separately.
- [ ] Vehicle *in operation* elevates the class (14-34.1(b)) — check.

**Assault on a female — G.S. 14-33(c)(2)** *(Case 10)*
- [ ] Male defendant **at least 18 years old** is an element. Confirm both sex
      and age from the report; if age is absent, it is a fact gap.

**Assault by pointing a gun — G.S. 14-34 (N.C.P.I. 208.85)** *(Case 10)*
- [ ] "In fun or otherwise" means the *reason* for pointing is irrelevant — it
      is NOT an element requiring playfulness. The elements are (1) defendant
      pointed a gun at the victim, (2) intentionally. Loaded/unloaded
      irrelevant. (The original study's worst single-element error was reading
      "in fun or otherwise" as an element.)

**AWDW / AWDWIKISI — G.S. 14-32, 14-33 (N.C.P.I. 208.10 et seq.)**
*(Cases 4, 6)*
- [ ] Serious injury: fact question — see Injuries sweep above.
- [ ] Intent to kill: needs supporting facts beyond the assault itself
      (multiple shots, close range, statements, pursuit). State them.
- [ ] Deadly weapon character of the object used (hands? knife? vehicle?).
- [ ] Distinguish the misdemeanor/felony assault variants as separate offenses
      with their own elements — never as "aggravating factors."

**Possession offenses — G.S. 90-95, firearm-by-felon, etc.** *(Case 8)*
- [ ] Attribute each item to a specific defendant: exactly where was the item
      found, and where was that defendant in relation to it?
- [ ] Actual vs. constructive possession — control over the place where the
      item was found.
- [ ] For vehicle stops: what did the officer observe *before* the escalation
      (baggie in shoe) — supports reasonable suspicion and the possession
      inference.
- [ ] Multi-defendant cases: specify which defendant faces which drug count;
      never lump "drugs were found."

**Conspiracy — N.C.P.I. 202.80** *(Cases 2, 5)*
- [ ] Agreement is the heart of the charge: statements ("run that"),
      coordinated conduct, joint arrival/flight. Name the facts showing
      agreement, not just parallel presence.

**Resisting officers — G.S. 14-223** *(Case 8)*
- [ ] Lawfulness of the officer's order/duty; the specific acts of resistance
      (verbal refusal, physical resistance, flight).
